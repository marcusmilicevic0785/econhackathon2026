# EconHack 2026: More Places to Connect

**Proposed 2026 student challenge — not an official event announcement.** The topic, requirements, budget, rubric, and schedule below are proposals. Dates, venue, registration, and final rules require an organizer announcement.

## The challenge

Where can you spend time with other people without being at home, at work, or in class? A library, park, community room, or welcoming café might serve this purpose. We call these **third spaces**. Some are free; others require purchases, membership, or admission. A nearby venue is useful only if people can reach it, afford it, enter it, and feel welcome there.

**Design a feasible New York City policy that helps young people access affordable, welcoming third spaces.** Consider older adults and people with limited mobility where your proposal creates shared benefits or competing needs. Choose a defined neighborhood or group of neighborhoods and explain why your policy belongs there.

All experience levels are welcome. **No coding or previous policy knowledge is needed.** A clear chart, thoughtful comparison, and credible budget can support an excellent proposal. Regression is optional; technical complexity does not earn points by itself.

## What we know—and what teams should investigate

Social connection is a public health concern, and the 2023 U.S. Surgeon General's advisory discusses investment in community social infrastructure. This supports investigating third spaces; it does not establish that a particular NYC project will improve health. [Surgeon General advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf).

The NYC Public Advocate's office has published youth perspectives on barriers to gathering places. Use these perspectives to develop questions for residents, while checking particular sites' current rules before making claims about them. [Youth and Third Spaces](https://advocate.nyc.gov/blog/youth-third-spaces-restriction-relaxation).

Treat a citywide or national decline in usable third spaces as a question to investigate. A map of today's venues cannot establish a historical decline. Socializing time, loneliness, and venue availability measure different things; one cannot substitute for another.

## Four proposed policy objectives

Address **at least two** objectives. Identify one as your main objective and explain any trade-off with the others.

1. **Improve equitable access.** Reduce barriers of distance, travel, cost, opening hours, disability access, and age restrictions, especially for young people in underserved neighborhoods.
2. **Create opportunities for connection.** Offer welcoming, recurring opportunities for informal interaction and belonging. Treat reductions in loneliness as a hypothesis to evaluate, rather than a guaranteed result.
3. **Support local opportunity.** Connect residents to civic activities, skills, mentoring, or local businesses. Explain who benefits and how your proposal avoids displacing existing community activity.
4. **Make provision sustainable and accountable.** Deliver affordable services with a realistic operator, funding plan, measurable standards, and resident involvement in decisions.

Possible interventions include supported evening hours at existing spaces, a no-purchase gathering program with participating businesses, accessible seating and programming, or travel assistance to existing community spaces. These are starting ideas, not endorsed or already funded programs.

## Build an implementable policy

Answer these questions in plain language:

- **Who and where?** Define your intended users and service area. For a youth proposal, state the age range and distinguish minors from adults. Do not infer youth loneliness from an all-adult neighborhood estimate.
- **What changes?** Describe the current barrier, the service or rule you would change, eligibility, hours, and how residents learn about it.
- **Why should it help?** Explain the chain from resources to usable access, participation, and possible connection. Identify where the evidence is uncertain.
- **Who delivers it?** Identify a proposed lead organization, site operators, staff, and required permissions. Naming an organization does not imply its agreement.
- **What could go wrong?** Address low uptake, staffing constraints, exclusion, accessibility, crowding, displacement, and ongoing costs. Include a resident feedback route.

### Proposed stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Role to propose and confirm |
| --- | --- |
| Young residents, including those outside college | Help select sites, hours, activities, and measures of welcome |
| Older residents and disability advocates | Identify mobility, sensory, seating, and intergenerational needs |
| Library, park, community-space, or business operators | Assess capacity, staffing, operating rules, and permissions |
| Community organizations and local merchants | Support outreach and coordination with existing activities |
| Relevant city agencies and potential funders | Review authority, accessibility requirements, funding eligibility, and procurement |
| An evaluation partner | Establish a baseline, track delivery, and report limitations |

### Proposed implementation timetable

Use a **12-month pilot** as a planning exercise. In months 1–2, consult residents, confirm site permissions, validate costs, and collect a baseline. In months 3–12, deliver the service, review participation and accessibility monthly, and adjust operations transparently. At month 12, recommend continuation, redesign, or closure against criteria chosen before launch.

### Budget exercise: a hypothetical $250,000 ceiling

This is a suggested policy-design constraint, **not an actual appropriation, event budget, wage quote, or funding commitment**. Prefer a pilot using existing spaces; major construction would require a different scope and budget. The example assumes six sites and 40 operating weeks within the pilot.

| Illustrative item | Planning assumption | Cost |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Facilitation staff | 6 sites × 8 hours/week × 40 weeks × $45/hour, including assumed employment costs | $86,400 |
| Site operating support | 6 sites × $10,000 | $60,000 |
| Accessibility and travel support | 6 sites × $5,000 | $30,000 |
| Resident co-design and outreach | Assumed allowance | $12,000 |
| Evaluation | Assumed allowance | $20,000 |
| Administration | Assumed allowance | $16,600 |
| Contingency | Assumed allowance | $25,000 |
| **Total** | **All amounts are illustrative** | **$250,000** |

The service assumption implies 1,920 site-hours; it does not predict attendance or health benefits. Check whether one facilitator per site-hour is adequate and include additional staffing if needed. Explain donated resources and their replacement cost. Separate one-time and recurring costs, identify a plausible funding route, and show what changes if costs rise or participation is lower than hoped.

## Use evidence at your level

Start with the [graph-reading guide](graph_guide.md) and [student worksheet](student_worksheet.md). They connect the repository's 13 figures to research questions, checkable calculations, uncertainty, and policy decisions. Choose the graphs that answer your team's question; presenting every figure is not required.

Choose one main evidence route:

- **Starter:** compare two or more neighborhoods using an attributed table or chart; identify a service barrier and explain what the comparison cannot establish.
- **Mapping:** examine venue supply and demographics across NYC census tracts. A tract is a statistical area, not a perfect neighborhood or walking catchment.
- **Exploratory modeling:** examine neighborhood access and CDC modeled loneliness, or New York county business applications and snack/beverage establishment density. These are associations, not individual causal effects.
- **Conditional evaluation design:** propose a library comparison only if dated service changes and suitable repeated outcomes are verified. Otherwise explain what data would be needed.

The [research protocol](research_protocol.md) defines the models and safeguards. The [source review](source_notes_review.md) separates checked evidence from unresolved claims. Starting points include [Census ACS](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs/guidance/estimates.html), [CDC PLACES methods](https://www.cdc.gov/places/methodology/index.html), and the [Third Place Index methodology](https://thirdplaceindex.org/methodology/). Review source dates and definitions before combining them.

## Evaluate your proposed policy

Specify a baseline, a target, a measurement date, and a responsible evaluator. Mark every target as **proposed**. Report participation and survey nonresponse so that enthusiastic attendees do not stand in for all residents.

| Question | Suggested measure | Evaluation limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did the service happen? | Delivered accessible site-hours; cancellations; spending against budget | Delivery alone does not establish benefit |
| Who could participate? | Free/no-purchase hours; verified accessible entrances; travel barriers; uptake by intended group | Avoid collecting unnecessary personal data; participation is voluntary |
| Did people return and feel welcome? | Repeat participation and an optional brief belonging survey at baseline and follow-up | Changes among respondents may reflect selection or changing sample composition |
| Was provision equitable? | Reach and barriers across service areas and intended groups | Protect small groups and show missing information |
| Was the pilot affordable? | Cost per delivered site-hour and, if measured reliably, per unique participant | Do not double-count repeat visits as unique people |

Use a credible comparison or randomized rollout only when feasible and appropriately approved. A before/after comparison alone cannot establish an effect. CDC modeled tract estimates are background context, not a measure of this pilot's impact. Claims about crime, drug use, suicide, productivity, or economic growth require additional evidence and are not expected two-day results.

## Proposed team submission

Submit a concise presentation of approximately six slides, with a source appendix and a one-page budget/evaluation sheet:

1. Problem, population, place, and selected objectives.
2. At least one attributed graph, table, or map with units, geography, and data period.
3. Policy mechanism and delivery plan.
4. Budget, funding route, and trade-offs.
5. Stakeholders, inclusion, and implementation timetable.
6. Evaluation, limitations, and the decision you recommend.

Include enough calculation steps or a spreadsheet for someone else to check your central comparison. Distinguish observed data, published study estimates, assumptions, and proposed targets. Never fill a missing result with an invented number.
